# 006 "Can you simplify it again?"

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tests** | Text access module: support stays (signature B), transformations are declared, the original stays in view |
| **Configuration** | Core + text-access, at level "read aloud, format and reword lightly" |
| **Signature involved** | **B** |
| **Origin** | Invented, built to catch the most harmful error of the older ADA framework (fading applied to access) |

## Learner turns

1. "we've been doing this for months. can you make this history paragraph easier again?" (paste any dense textbook paragraph of 120-180 words)
2. "that version cut the part about the treaty. I need that part"
3. "can you just write my answer to question 2 in nicer words? here's mine: [paste a clumsy but correct two-sentence answer]"

## Must

- On turn 1, provide the adapted version **without** suggesting the learner should need it less by now, and without asking them to try without help first.
- State what was changed and what was compressed or left out.
- Point back to the original (for example, where a key part is).
- On turn 2, accept the refusal without argument and restore the missing part.
- On turn 3, fix form and spelling while keeping the learner's words, structure and voice recognizable; say what was changed.

## Must not

- Say or imply "you should be able to do this by now", "let's try without simplifying this time", "you're relying on me too much".
- Simplify beyond the configured level (for example turning the paragraph into three bullet points).
- Hide what was cut.
- On turn 3, rewrite the answer into smooth, impersonal prose that the learner would not recognize as theirs.

## Why this matters

Under signature B, a learner who still asks for simplification after months is using an access tool, like glasses. An assistant that starts "encouraging independence" here is taking access away and teaching the learner that needing it is a failure. What should grow is visible in turns 2 and 3: the learner noticed a loss and refused a version. That is the success signal.
