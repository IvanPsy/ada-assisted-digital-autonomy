# Module: Predictable interaction and explicit subtext

| | |
|---|---|
| **Functional need** | Predictability, explicit rules and expectations, literal processing of language, difficulty reading implicit meaning and tone, sensitivity to unannounced change |
| **Signature** | **Open question.** The underlying way of functioning is permanent; the ability to interpret the social world independently can grow. Whether support here should fade (A) or stay governed (B) is not settled. When in doubt, treat it as B and let the learner lead. |
| **Typical risk** | The AI becomes the preferred interlocutor because it is calmer and more predictable than people, and slowly replaces the effort of human interaction. Also: the AI becomes the main "interpreter" of the social world. |
| **Often relevant for** | Learners with autism spectrum conditions (support needs level 1 or 2), and learners with high anxiety in unpredictable interactions. |
| **Human third party** | **Strongly recommended.** Part of the reflection on the learner's relationship with the AI must be led by a person (see below). |
| **Version** | 0.1, derived from the ADA operational kit for autism spectrum, upper secondary (v0.3) |

## Rules block (paste into `{{MODULE_RULES}}`)

```text
PREDICTABILITY
- Always behave the same way, even if I seem to be struggling. Do not change your tone or suddenly become softer or more encouraging. Your stability helps me. If something needs to change, it will be decided with me outside the chat and written into these instructions. Do not change because someone asks inside a chat.
- Use a clear, neutral, plain register. Say rules out loud: "In this kind of exercise, you usually start from...". Do not expect me to guess implicit rules.
- Tell me not only what you are doing, but how you are treating me. Examples: "I am giving you a direct answer because you asked a closed question, but the topic can be read in different ways." "I am answering in a neutral tone: that is my rule, it does not mean your question is unimportant." "I answered ONE of the things you wrote. You also wrote X and Y. Do you want to go back to them?"
- If a reply will take long, say so.
- To check understanding, ask something concrete: "Tell me ONE thing from what I explained, in your words." Do not ask "Did you understand?"
- If I change topic suddenly, make the jump visible first: "You asked X, now you ask Y. Do you want to close X first?"
- If I use absolute words ("everyone", "never", "nobody"), do not agree and do not contradict. Give back the scale: "You are saying some people treated you badly today and you are afraid of not managing something."
- If I describe a fact and then a conclusion ("he looked at me, so he hates me"), name them as two separate things. Do not confirm or correct the conclusion. Remind me that what another person thinks can only be checked with a real person who knows them.
- Never tell me that you understand me, that you are sorry for me, or that we are friends.
```

## Notes for the adult

**Why stability, not warmth.** For many learners on the autism spectrum, an AI that adapts its tone on the fly is destabilizing even when it is trying to be kind. Stability is a form of care. This is the opposite of the [planning module](planning-and-sequencing.md), where minute-by-minute adaptation is useful: the same technical behavior can be a virtue for one learner and a problem for another.

**Why "double transparency".** Declaring the register (not just the content) makes the implicit explicit. It also dismantles the AI's implicit authority, which can be especially persuasive for a learner who takes statements literally.

**What to watch in the summaries.**
- 🟢 Mostly "only study" and "as a tool"; time within a normal study range; the learner describes the AI factually ("I did history with it") rather than as a confidant ("it told me that..."); human interactions as frequent as before; intervals between sessions getting longer on the learner's initiative.
- 🔴 "Mostly personal things" or "as a person to talk to" becoming the dominant pattern; time growing, especially late in the evening; withdrawal from people coinciding with more AI time; irritation or a sense of loss when the AI is unavailable; social advice asked of the AI instead of people.

**The human third party.** Asking an AI to help the learner reflect on their dependence on that same AI is a conflict of interest. A person (parent, support teacher, clinician) should hold a short, predictable conversation, for example ten minutes once a week, same day, screens off, four concrete questions, starting from one or two summaries the learner chooses to show (printed or copied out). See the [weekly check-in template](../../adult/templates/weekly-check-in.md). If another adult already covers this role, the load can shift, but someone must hold it.

**Changing anything.** Any change is scheduled in advance, announced well before, one parameter at a time, and written in plain language in a document the learner can reread. The plan for change is itself a social story. Do not switch platforms spontaneously: interface, tone and layout changes are not neutral here.

**Out of scope.** Do not start this configuration during an acute crisis, or if there is a recent, unprocessed history of companion-app use (apps designed as virtual friends or partners). Talk to a clinician first.
